Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Does Your Drain Actually Need?

Written by the Alpharetta Express Plumbing team · Drains & Sewer · Published 2026.

A clogged drain gets you one of two common fixes: snaking, sometimes called cabling, or hydro jetting. They're not interchangeable. Each is built for a different kind of problem, and picking the wrong one either wastes money or leaves the real cause untouched.

What Snaking Actually Does

A drain snake is a flexible metal cable fed into the pipe, either by hand for smaller jobs or with a motorized cable machine for tougher ones. The end of the cable breaks through or hooks onto whatever is blocking the line, then pulls it free or punches a channel through it. It's fast, effective for a single, localized clog, and the standard first step for most everyday drain calls.

What Hydro Jetting Actually Does

Hydro jetting uses a high-pressure water stream, delivered through a specialized nozzle, to scour the full interior wall of the pipe. Rather than punching a hole through a clog the way a cable does, it clears grease, scale, and debris from the entire diameter of the line. That's a meaningfully different result: a jetted line is clean along its full length, not just where the clog happened to be.

When Snaking Is The Right Call

  • A single, first-time clog in a sink, tub, or toilet.
  • A localized blockage with a clear, recent cause, like hair or a foreign object.
  • A quick, lower-cost fix when there's no history of recurring problems.

When Hydro Jetting Makes More Sense

  • A clog that keeps coming back after cabling, suggesting buildup along the pipe walls rather than a single blockage.
  • Grease buildup in a kitchen line, especially in a home that cooks often or a light commercial kitchen.
  • Root intrusion in an older sewer line, which jetting clears more thoroughly than a cable can.
  • A main line with a known history of slow drainage across multiple fixtures.

If you're not sure which category your situation falls into, a camera inspection before either method settles it. This is standard practice before we recommend hydro jetting specifically, since it confirms the pipe can handle the pressure and that jetting is actually the right tool for what's really going on.

Cost And Time Differences

Snaking is generally quicker and less expensive, which is part of why it's the default first approach for a routine clog. Hydro jetting takes longer and costs more, but it addresses buildup along the entire pipe rather than just the blockage point, which is why it's worth the difference for a genuinely recurring problem.

A Real Local Factor

Established Alpharetta neighborhoods with mature trees see more root intrusion in older sewer lines than newer subdivisions with younger landscaping. That's a genuine, physical reason jetting comes up more often in some parts of town than others, not a generic upsell. If a standard drain cleaning visit turns up signs of root intrusion, we'll tell you plainly rather than just cabling the same spot again next time.

We see this pattern often in Norcross's older, tree-lined sections, where original clay and cast-iron sewer lines are more common than in newer construction elsewhere in our coverage area.

What Happens If You Pick The Wrong One

Snaking a line that actually needs jetting doesn't fix the underlying problem. It punches through the immediate blockage, water flows again, and the buildup along the pipe walls is still there waiting to cause the same clog again in a few weeks or months. This is the pattern behind a lot of repeat service calls for what looks like the same issue.

Jetting a line that only needed a simple cable job isn't harmful, but it does cost more than necessary for a problem that a quicker, cheaper method would have solved just as well. Neither mistake is dangerous, but both cost you money or time you didn't need to spend.

How We Actually Decide

We start with the simplest explanation that fits what you're describing. A single clogged sink with no history of problems gets cabled first. A recurring issue, a kitchen line with heavy grease buildup, or a sewer line with known root intrusion gets a camera inspection before we commit to either method, so the recommendation is based on what we actually see rather than a guess.

Can You Do Either Of These Yourself?

A basic hand-held drain snake for a slow sink is reasonable for a homeowner to try. Motorized cabling and hydro jetting both require equipment and training most households don't have, and using the wrong pressure or technique on an already-compromised pipe can turn a clog into a bigger repair. For anything beyond a simple hand-snake attempt, it's worth calling rather than risking it.

What A Typical Visit Looks Like

  • We ask about the history: is this a first-time clog or a repeat issue.
  • For anything with a recurring pattern, we run a camera to see the actual condition of the line.
  • We recommend cabling or jetting based on what the camera or the situation actually shows.
  • We confirm the fix worked before we consider the visit complete.

How Often A Jetted Line Needs It Again

A line that's been properly jetted, especially one with a grease or root intrusion history, often goes much longer between issues than one that's only ever been cabled. That's not a guarantee against future problems, since roots can grow back and grease can rebuild over time, but it resets the line closer to its original condition rather than leaving buildup in place.

For a property with a genuine recurring pattern, a periodic jetting schedule, done proactively rather than reactively, is sometimes worth discussing rather than waiting for the next backup to force the issue.

If you're not sure which category applies to your situation, describing the history to us over the phone, first-time versus recurring, kitchen versus bathroom, is usually enough to point us toward the right starting recommendation before a technician even arrives.

Common Questions

Can hydro jetting damage old pipes?

It depends on pipe material and condition, which is why we camera-inspect first. We won't recommend jetting on a line that can't safely handle the pressure.

Is snaking bad for my pipes if used repeatedly?

Occasional cabling is fine, but if you're calling for the same clog every few months, that's a sign the underlying cause isn't being addressed, and jetting or further diagnosis is worth considering.

Do I need a camera inspection every time?

Not for a routine, first-time clog. It's most useful when a clog recurs or when jetting is being considered for a line with an unclear history.
