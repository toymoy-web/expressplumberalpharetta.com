Repiping an Older Home: What to Actually Expect

Written by the Alpharetta Express Plumbing team · Home Maintenance · Published 2026.

Repiping an older home sounds intimidating, and the word alone makes some homeowners picture their whole house torn open for weeks. In practice, a well-planned repipe is a lot more contained than that, and knowing what actually happens ahead of time makes the whole thing far less stressful.

Why A Home Reaches This Point

Repiping usually isn't the first fix anyone reaches for. It becomes the right call after a pattern shows up: repeated pinhole leaks in galvanized pipe, or a home with polybutylene pipe from the 1980s and 90s that's known to fail regardless of visible symptoms yet. One or two isolated leaks are usually just repairs. A pattern of them is what points to the pipe itself being the problem.

The Planning Stage

Before anything gets opened, we map the job: which walls and ceilings need access, where the new lines will run, and how to route them with the least possible disruption. This planning step is what keeps a repipe from turning into more demolition than necessary.

What Actually Gets Opened

  • Small, targeted openings in walls near existing fixtures, not entire wall sections.
  • Access points in ceilings below upper-floor bathrooms, where relevant.
  • In slab homes, sometimes an overhead routing approach through attic space instead of breaking the slab at all.

The goal throughout is opening only what's needed to run the new lines, not gutting rooms. Most homeowners are surprised at how contained the actual openings are compared to what they pictured going in.

Material Choice: PEX Or Copper

Both are approved under the Georgia State Minimum Standard Plumbing Code and are durable, code-compliant choices. PEX costs less and is more flexible to route through tight spaces, which is why it's the more common choice for a full repipe. Copper has a longer track record and some homeowners prefer it on that basis. We'll walk through the tradeoff for your specific home rather than assume one is automatically right.

How Long It Actually Takes

Most single-family homes are repiped in a few days, not weeks. Water is off for portions of each day while work is underway, and we coordinate scheduling around your household's needs where possible. Multi-story homes or larger square footage naturally take longer than a smaller single-story house.

What Happens After The Pipe Is In

Once the new lines are run and pressure-tested, the openings get patched to a paintable finish. Final paint matching is typically on the homeowner unless arranged otherwise, since paint matching depends on your existing wall color and finish. A whole-home repipe project always includes this patching step as part of the job, not as an afterthought.

What A Repipe Does Not Include

The buried service line running from the meter or curb stop to your home is a separate scope, covered under water line repair and replacement rather than the interior repiping project. If both are needed, we'll scope them as related but distinct pieces of work.

A Local Note

Homes in Alpharetta built in the 1980s into the early 1990s are the ones most likely to still have polybutylene pipe, and it's worth having that checked even without a current leak, since the material's failure risk doesn't depend on visible symptoms.

What Signals This Is Actually Necessary

  • Two or more pinhole leak repairs on galvanized pipe within a year or two.
  • Confirmed polybutylene pipe from a 1980s or 90s build.
  • Consistently low water pressure throughout the house, not just at one fixture.
  • Discolored water from multiple taps, suggesting corrosion throughout the system rather than one bad section.

Cost Factors Worth Understanding

Home size and layout are the biggest cost drivers, since more square footage and more fixtures mean more pipe and more access points. Material choice matters too, with PEX generally costing less than copper for a comparable scope. Slab homes and multi-story layouts can affect access and therefore labor time compared to a simpler single-story home with basement access.

Living In The House During The Project

Most households stay in the home throughout a repipe. The main inconvenience is water being off for portions of each day while a section of the system is worked on, which we try to schedule around your household's routine where possible. Bathrooms are typically restored to working order by the end of each work day rather than left non-functional overnight.

How This Differs From A Partial Repipe

Sometimes a home only needs certain sections repiped, like just the kitchen and one bathroom, rather than the whole house. This is a smaller, less expensive project than a full repipe, and it's worth discussing whether your specific pattern of issues points to a partial or full scope before committing to either.

Insurance And Repiping

Some homeowner's insurance policies view a documented repipe favorably, since it addresses a known risk factor for future water damage claims. It's worth asking your insurance provider whether completed repiping affects your policy or premium, since this varies by insurer and isn't something we can speak to directly.

What we can tell you honestly is whether your specific situation, based on pipe material, age, and repair history, is at the point where a repipe makes more sense than continuing to patch individual leaks as they come up.

Either way, we'll give you a clear, honest answer based on what we actually see, not a default recommendation toward whichever job is larger.

Working With Your Homeowner's Association

If you're in an HOA-governed community, it's worth checking whether exterior work like accessing a crawlspace or running new lines through shared walls in a townhome setting requires any notification or approval. Most repiping stays entirely within your own unit's interior, but it's a quick check worth making before work begins rather than after.

We're happy to walk through the specifics with your HOA directly if that makes the process smoother for you.

A short conversation early in the process usually resolves any concerns before they become a scheduling issue.

There's rarely a reason this can't be worked out smoothly with a little advance notice.

What To Do If You Notice A Leak While Waiting

  • Shut off the water at the fixture or the main valve if the leak is active.
  • Move furniture and belongings away from the affected area.
  • Document the leak with photos in case it's relevant to an insurance claim.
  • Call us to move up your scheduled visit if a new leak appears before your repipe date.

None of this means you need to panic between now and your scheduled repipe. It just means knowing the basic steps if a new leak shows up before the project starts.

Common Questions

Do I need to move out during a repipe?

Most homeowners stay. Water is off for portions of each day, and we coordinate scheduling around your household's routine.

Will you match my paint when patching the walls?

We patch to a paintable finish. Final paint matching is typically on the homeowner, since it depends on your specific existing paint.

How do I know if my home actually needs a repipe versus a repair?

A pattern of repeated leaks, or known polybutylene pipe, are the clearest signals. A single isolated leak is usually just a repair.
